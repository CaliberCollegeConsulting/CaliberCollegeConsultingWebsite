The 4 D's: Essay Topics to Approach With Caution

By Alex Munoz · September 2026

Every fall, admissions readers sit down with thousands of college essays, and a handful of topics show up again and again. In admissions circles, they're sometimes called the "4 D's": death, destruction, disease, and divorce.

None of these topics are off-limits. Some of the most powerful essays I've read tackle exactly these subjects. But they're also some of the easiest topics to get wrong, which is why it's worth understanding the risk before you sit down to write.

Why the 4 D's Are Risky

Admissions readers see so many essays about loss, crisis, illness, and family change that these topics have become, in a sense, the default college essay. That doesn't mean your experience isn't real or significant. It means the bar for making your essay stand out is higher, because the reader has likely read a version of this story before.

The other risk is structural. Essays about the 4 D's tend to spend most of their word count describing what happened, the event itself, and very little space on what the writer actually learned or how they changed. Admissions officers aren't grading you on the hardest thing that ever happened to you. They're trying to understand who you are and how you think, and that only comes through in the reflection, not the retelling.

The 4 D's, One at a Time

Death

Writing only about grief can unintentionally center the loss itself rather than you. It can also read as reaching for sympathy rather than sharing a genuine story. If you choose to write about the death of someone close to you, the essay needs to move past the loss itself and show what you learned and how you've grown because of it, not just what happened.

Destruction

Essays about a natural disaster, accident, or other crisis often spend paragraph after paragraph describing the event in vivid detail, then run out of room for reflection. Spend less time narrating what happened and more time on how you responded: the decisions you made, the person you had to become in that moment, and what it revealed about your character.

Disease

An essay about your own illness, or a family member's, can come across as an explanation for a weaker transcript or a gap in activities rather than a window into who you are. If health is central to your story, focus on the specific insight, habit, or perspective the experience gave you, not just the diagnosis or treatment timeline.

Divorce

Divorce is one of the most common college essay topics, which means a generic take on it can blend into hundreds of others an admissions reader sees that season. The way to make it yours is specificity: the one detail, moment, or realization that only you would notice. General statements about "learning to be independent" won't set you apart. A precise, personal detail will.

It's Not About Avoiding These Topics

To be clear, none of the 4 D's are topics you need to avoid entirely. If one of these experiences genuinely shaped who you are, it's fair game, and it can make for a compelling essay. The goal isn't to steer away from hard experiences. It's to make sure the essay is doing what a college essay needs to do: showing growth, reflection, and the specific details that make your story only yours, rather than retelling a difficult event and stopping there.
